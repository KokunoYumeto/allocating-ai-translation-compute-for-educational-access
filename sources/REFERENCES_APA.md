# APA-style literature register

## Scope and use

This register supports the from-scratch study of globally equitable allocation of AI translation compute for educational access. It records what each source can support and, just as importantly, what it cannot support. It is not a substitute for the study's target-level evidence register, population tables, scoring specification, or exact variety/script audit.

Source-type labels are descriptive:

- **Peer-reviewed synthesis**: systematic review, meta-analysis, or major peer-reviewed review.
- **Peer-reviewed empirical**: an original peer-reviewed study with a defined sample and method.
- **Intergovernmental primary**: a report, standard, database, or legal instrument issued by an intergovernmental body.
- **Technical standard**: a normative interoperability or accessibility specification.
- **Professional/advocacy primary**: a statement or estimate issued by the relevant representative organization; useful within its declared scope but not a population census or independent efficacy study.
- **Institutional secondary**: a published institutional synthesis or later reproduction of an earlier estimate.

## Language of instruction, multilingual education, and learning

Nakamura, P., Molotsky, A., Castro Zarzur, R., Ranjit, V., Haddad, Y., & de Hoop, T. (2023). Language of instruction in schools in low- and middle-income countries: A systematic review. *Campbell Systematic Reviews, 19*(4), e1351. https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1351

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed synthesis.
- **Supports:** The review included 45 high-quality studies (11 randomized controlled trials, 11 quasi-experimental, 7 cross-sectional, and 16 qualitative studies). Its meta-analysis reports positive effects of mother-tongue interventions on several literacy outcomes, while also documenting heterogeneity, implementation dependence, regional concentration, and publication-bias concerns.
- **Does not support:** A universal effect size for every language, stage, subject, or education system; the proposition that translation alone reproduces a complete multilingual-education intervention.

UNESCO. (2025). *Languages matter: Global guidance on multilingual education*. UNESCO. https://doi.org/10.54675/MLIO7101

- **Type:** Intergovernmental primary guidance and synthesis.
- **Supports:** UNESCO's current policy framing that learners should receive education in languages they understand; the reported broad context that 40% of people globally lack access to education in a language they speak and understand fluently; multilingual curriculum, materials, assessment, teacher, and policy considerations.
- **Does not support:** A current named-language census, a claim that 40% receive no education at all, or use of 40% as a target-level denominator without separate language- and territory-specific evidence.

UNESCO Institute for Statistics, & UNICEF. (2015). *Fixing the broken promise of education for all: Findings from the global initiative on out-of-school children*. UNESCO Institute for Statistics. https://iite.unesco.org/files/news/639224/UNESCO-OOSCreport-Full-web.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental institutional synthesis.
- **Supports:** The historical statement that an estimated 2.3 billion people—nearly 40% of the then-world population—lacked access to education in their own language, explicitly attributed to Walter and Benson (2012).
- **Does not support:** A 2026 population estimate, a named list of affected languages, or a causal estimate derived from individual learners.

Walter, S. L., & Benson, C. (2012). Language policy and medium of instruction in formal education. In B. Spolsky (Ed.), *The Cambridge handbook of language policy* (pp. 278–300). Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511979026.017

- **Type:** Scholarly book chapter; primary source for the historical language-of-instruction tabulation.
- **Supports:** The historical method and aggregate estimates reported in the chapter, including the table on p. 283. A later published reproduction reports that 45 of the 97 languages with more than 10 million speakers were not used in schools and together represented more than one billion speakers.
- **Access caveat:** The publisher's public metadata confirms authorship, chapter title, pages, year, and DOI, but the full table is paywalled. No printed appendix naming all 97 languages was located or verified. The aggregate must not be reverse-engineered into a named list.
- **Does not support:** Current language populations, current education coverage, or target-level rankings.

World Bank. (2021). *Loud and clear: Effective language of instruction policies for learning*. World Bank. https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/517851626203470278/pdf/Loud-and-Clear-Effective-Language-of-Instruction-Policies-For-Learning.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental primary modeling and policy synthesis.
- **Supports:** The report's estimate that 37% of students in low- and middle-income countries—about 370 million of roughly one billion students in its analysis—were taught in a language other than the language they best spoke and understood. It separately models how use of roughly 550 additional languages could expand first-language instruction coverage.
- **Does not support:** Treating the 37% student estimate as identical to UNESCO's 40% global-population statement, or treating the modeled 550-language scenario as current observed coverage.

## The three superficially similar language counts

Crawford, M., Raheel, N., Korochkina, M., & Rastle, K. (2025). Inadequate foundational decoding skills constrain global literacy goals for pupils in low- and middle-income countries. *Nature Human Behaviour, 9*, 74–83. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02028-x

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical synthesis of assessment datasets.
- **Supports:** The article's analytical sample: 230 Early Grade Reading Assessment surveys, 694 subsurveys, 48 countries, 96 languages, 22,656 schools, and 526,862 pupils learning to read in alphabetic writing systems. The complete underlying database is larger and includes additional writing systems; the reported article analysis is restricted to alphabetic systems.
- **Does not support:** Identifying those 96 assessment languages with Walter and Benson's 97 languages over 10 million speakers; deriving UNESCO's 40% estimate; or using the 96-language sample as a global language-need census.

Spolsky, B. (2021). *Rethinking language policy*. Edinburgh University Press. https://doi.org/10.3366/edinburgh/9781474485463.001.0001

- **Type:** Scholarly monograph; institutional secondary reproduction of the Walter–Benson aggregate.
- **Supports:** The published statement that 45 of 97 languages with more than 10 million speakers were not used in schools and together represented more than one billion speakers, attributed to Walter and Benson (2012, p. 283).
- **Does not support:** A named list of the 97 languages or current target-level values.

## Learning poverty and education stages

UNESCO Institute for Statistics. (2012). *International standard classification of education: ISCED 2011*. UNESCO Institute for Statistics. https://uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/documents/international-standard-classification-of-education-isced-2011-en.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental classification standard.
- **Supports:** Comparable education levels from early childhood through doctoral or equivalent education (ISCED 0–8), with explicit classification criteria.
- **Does not support:** Assuming identical ages, grades, curricula, credentials, or learning needs across national systems.

UNESCO Institute for Statistics. (2014). *ISCED fields of education and training 2013 (ISCED-F 2013): Manual to accompany the International Standard Classification of Education 2011*. UNESCO Institute for Statistics. https://uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/documents/isced-fields-of-education-and-training-2013-en.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental classification standard.
- **Supports:** Cross-national subject-field classification.
- **Does not support:** A normative priority ordering among fields or a conclusion that a field is locally missing.

UNESCO Institute for Statistics. (2020). *Global proficiency framework for reading: Grades 1 to 9*. UNESCO Institute for Statistics. https://gaml.uis.unesco.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2021/03/Global-Proficiency-Framework-Reading.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental assessment framework.
- **Supports:** A common framework for reading proficiency across grades 1–9.
- **Does not support:** Curriculum completeness, equivalence of orthographies, or the adequacy of one universal decoding benchmark for every language.

World Bank. (2022). *The state of global learning poverty: 2022 update*. World Bank. https://www.unicef.org/media/122921/file/State%20of%20Learning%20Poverty%202022.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental modeling and monitoring report.
- **Supports:** The report's simulated post-COVID estimate that 70% of 10-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries could not read and understand a simple text, and the report's stated assumptions and uncertainty.
- **Does not support:** A current census, a global all-age illiteracy rate, a language-specific rate, or a direct estimate of translation demand.

World Bank. (2024). *Learning Poverty Global Database: Historical data and sub-components* [Data set]. World Bank. https://datacatalog.worldbank.org/search/dataset/0038947/learning-poverty-global-database-historical-data-and-sub-components

- **Type:** Intergovernmental primary dataset.
- **Supports:** Country-level learning-poverty indicators and their school-deprivation and learning-deprivation components where reported.
- **Does not support:** Language- or variety-level inference without a separate disaggregation source; values for countries with missing or non-comparable observations must not be silently imputed.

## Open educational resources and functional access

Clinton, V., & Khan, S. (2019). Efficacy of open textbook adoption on learning performance and course withdrawal rates: A meta-analysis. *AERA Open, 5*(3), 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419872212

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
- **Supports:** The evaluated evidence on open-textbook adoption, learning performance, and withdrawal in the included studies.
- **Does not support:** A global K–12 effect; language access; curriculum completeness; or the proposition that an open license by itself makes a resource usable offline or accessible to disabled learners.

Hilton, J., III. (2016). Open educational resources and college textbook choices: A review of research on efficacy and perceptions. *Educational Technology Research and Development, 64*(4), 573–590. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-016-9434-9

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed review.
- **Supports:** A 16-study review, predominantly in higher education, finding generally comparable learning outcomes and favorable cost/perception findings, while emphasizing design limitations.
- **Does not support:** Universal efficacy, a global educational-access effect size, or extrapolation from North American postsecondary settings to every stage and country.

Cho, K. W., & Permzadian, V. (2024). The impact of open educational resources on student achievement: A meta-analysis. *International Journal of Educational Research, 126*, 102365. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijer.2024.102365

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
- **Supports:** The meta-analysis of 26 studies on achievement-related outcomes in the included educational settings.
- **Does not support:** Treating openness, translation, access, pedagogy, device availability, and learning outcomes as one variable.

UNESCO. (2019). *Recommendation on open educational resources (OER)*. https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-educational-resources-oer

- **Type:** Intergovernmental normative instrument.
- **Supports:** The definition of OER as materials in the public domain or under an open license permitting no-cost access, reuse, repurposing, adaptation, and redistribution; policy actions on access, inclusion, accessibility, linguistic diversity, capacity, and sustainability.
- **Does not support:** A finding that a particular resource is complete, source-faithful, usable on a phone, available offline, pedagogically effective, or accessible in practice. Each property requires its own evidence.

## Connectivity, devices, electricity, and offline access

GSMA. (2025). *The state of mobile internet connectivity 2025: Overview report*. https://www.gsma.com/somic/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/The-State-of-Mobile-Internet-Connectivity-2025-Overview-Report.pdf

- **Type:** Industry-primary measurement report.
- **Supports:** GSMA's definitions and estimates for mobile coverage gaps, usage gaps, and device ownership/sharing among mobile users.
- **Does not support:** A general population census of Internet use or personal smartphone ownership; its denominators and definitions differ from ITU and UNICEF figures and must not be added to them.

International Energy Agency, International Renewable Energy Agency, United Nations Statistics Division, World Bank, & World Health Organization. (2025). *Tracking SDG 7: The energy progress report 2025*. World Bank. https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/energy/publication/tracking-sdg-7-the-energy-progress-report-2025

- **Type:** Intergovernmental joint monitoring report.
- **Supports:** The estimate that 666 million people lacked basic electricity access in 2023, including 565 million in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Does not support:** Reliable electricity, affordability, device ownership, charging frequency, bandwidth, or individual ability to use a digital educational resource.

International Telecommunication Union. (2025). *Measuring digital development: Facts and Figures 2025*. https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Statistics/Pages/facts/default.aspx

- **Type:** Intergovernmental primary statistical report.
- **Supports:** ITU's 2025 estimates that roughly 6 billion people were online and 2.2 billion were offline under its Internet-user definition.
- **Does not support:** Home connectivity, broadband quality, device ownership, electricity, digital skills, continuous access, or ability to download a large corpus.

UNICEF, & International Telecommunication Union. (2020). *How many children and young people have internet access at home? Estimating digital connectivity during the COVID-19 pandemic*. UNICEF. https://www.unicef.org/media/88381/file/How-many-children-and-young-people-have-internet-access-at-home-2020.pdf

- **Type:** Intergovernmental modeled estimate.
- **Supports:** The 2020 estimate that 1.3 billion school-age children aged 3–17 and 2.2 billion children and young people aged 25 or younger lacked Internet access at home.
- **Does not support:** Current access, access outside the home, personal-device ownership, or non-overlap with later ITU/GSMA estimates.

## Disability, signed languages, and accessible formats

Burton, M. J., Ramke, J., Marques, A. P., Bourne, R. R. A., Congdon, N., Jones, I., Tong, B. A. M. A., Arunga, S., Bachani, D., Bascaran, C., Bastawrous, A., Blanchet, K., Braithwaite, T., Buchan, J. C., Cairns, J., Cama, A., Chagunda, M., Chuluunkhuu, C., Cooper, A., … Faal, H. B. (2021). The Lancet Global Health Commission on global eye health: Vision beyond 2020. *The Lancet Global Health, 9*(4), e489–e551. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(20)30488-5

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed global commission and synthesis.
- **Supports:** Global estimates of blindness and moderate-to-severe vision impairment under the commission's definitions.
- **Does not support:** Treating every person with visual impairment as a screen-reader or Braille user, or assigning all to one accessible-format intervention.

GBD 2019 Hearing Loss Collaborators. (2021). Hearing loss prevalence and years lived with disability, 1990–2019: Findings from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019. *The Lancet, 397*(10278), 996–1009. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(21)00516-X

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed global burden analysis.
- **Supports:** Hearing-loss prevalence estimates under defined audiometric thresholds.
- **Does not support:** The number of sign-language users, Deaf-community membership, caption dependence, or demand for one particular sign language.

United Nations. (2006). *Convention on the rights of persons with disabilities*, Article 24. https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-24-education.html

- **Type:** International legal instrument.
- **Supports:** State obligations concerning accessible education, Braille, sign language, appropriate modes and means of communication, and education in the most appropriate languages and modes for the individual.
- **Does not support:** A prevalence estimate or proof that a particular edition conforms to the Convention.

UNICEF. (2021). *Seen, counted, included: Using data to shed light on the well-being of children with disabilities*. https://data.unicef.org/resources/children-with-disabilities-report-2021/

- **Type:** Intergovernmental statistical synthesis.
- **Supports:** UNICEF's estimate of nearly 240 million children with disabilities, based on multiple functional domains.
- **Does not support:** A single-format beneficiary count; domains and support needs differ and overlap.

World Federation of the Deaf. (2014). *WFD statement on standardized sign language*. https://wfdeaf.org/resources/statement-on-standardised-sign-language/

- **Type:** Professional/advocacy primary statement.
- **Supports:** The representative organization's warning against imposed standardization and its emphasis on natural national/local sign languages.
- **Does not support:** Population prevalence or direct measurement of mutual intelligibility among sign languages.

World Federation of the Deaf. (n.d.). *Frequently asked questions*. https://wfdeaf.org/contact/faqs/

- **Type:** Professional/advocacy primary estimate.
- **Supports:** WFD's explicit statement that no official global count exists and its estimate that more than 70 million people belong to signing Deaf communities using more than 200 national sign languages.
- **Does not support:** A census, one global sign language, or assigning the entire estimate to any single signed-language edition.

World Health Organization. (2023, March 7). *Disability*. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health

- **Type:** Intergovernmental fact sheet and synthesis.
- **Supports:** WHO's estimate that about 1.3 billion people—16% of the global population—experience significant disability.
- **Does not support:** A beneficiary count for any single accessibility format or diagnosis.

World Health Organization, & UNICEF. (2022). *Global report on assistive technology*. World Health Organization. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240049451

- **Type:** Intergovernmental joint synthesis.
- **Supports:** The estimate that more than 2.5 billion people need at least one assistive product, with large access gaps.
- **Does not support:** Treating all assistive-product need as a need for accessible educational text; included products range from spectacles and hearing aids to mobility and communication devices.

World Wide Web Consortium. (2023). *EPUB accessibility 1.1*. https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-a11y-11/

- **Type:** Technical standard.
- **Supports:** Normative requirements for accessible EPUB publications and discoverable accessibility metadata.
- **Does not support:** Actual conformance without file-level testing, or a population estimate.

World Wide Web Consortium. (2023). *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2*. https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

- **Type:** Technical standard.
- **Supports:** Testable success criteria for web accessibility.
- **Does not support:** A claim that a downloadable PDF, HTML page, or repository is accessible merely because it is public.

## Low-resource machine translation, multilingual AI, and compute

Amano, T., Ramírez-Castañeda, V., Berdejo-Espinola, V., Borokini, I., Chowdhury, S., Golivets, M., González-Trujillo, J. D., Montaño-Centellas, F., Paudel, K., & White, R. L. (2023). The manifold costs of being a non-native English speaker in science. *PLOS Biology, 21*(7), e3002184. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002184

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical survey of 908 environmental-science researchers across eight nationalities.
- **Supports:** Reported additional time and effort for non-native English-speaking researchers reading, writing, presenting, publishing, and disseminating science in English; the study's sample and uncertainty, including the distinction between low- and moderate-English-proficiency nationality groups.
- **Does not support:** A universal percentage of all researchers or learners who lack academic English, a language-specific population denominator, or direct causal effects of a translated educational package.

Ahia, O., Kumar, S., Gonen, H., Kasai, J., Mortensen, D. R., Smith, N. A., & Tsvetkov, Y. (2023). Do all languages cost the same? Tokenization in the era of commercial language models. In H. Bouamor, J. Pino, & K. Bali (Eds.), *Proceedings of the 2023 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing* (pp. 9904–9923). Association for Computational Linguistics. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.614

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical computational-linguistics study.
- **Supports:** Language-dependent tokenization disparities across the evaluated commercial models and 22 languages.
- **Does not support:** A permanent universal multiplier for a language, exact cost for an untested model/corpus, or translation-quality equivalence.

Guerreiro, N. M., Alves, D. M., Waldendorf, J., Haddow, B., Birch, A., Colombo, P., & Martins, A. F. T. (2023). Hallucinations in large multilingual translation models. *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, 11*, 1500–1517. https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00615

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical computational-linguistics study.
- **Supports:** Analysis over more than 100 translation directions showing elevated hallucination risks in low-resource and out-of-English settings, and evidence that diverse fallback systems can mitigate certain pathologies.
- **Does not support:** A claim that every low-resource translation fails, that model scaling alone guarantees correctness, or that a nominally separate second model is independent when trained on similar data.

Haddow, B., Bawden, R., Miceli Barone, A. V., Helcl, J., & Birch, A. (2022). Survey of low-resource machine translation. *Computational Linguistics, 48*(3), 673–732. https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00446

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed synthesis.
- **Supports:** The state of low-resource MT, data scarcity, transfer, multilinguality, back-translation, unsupervised approaches, evaluation challenges, and large differences among language pairs.
- **Does not support:** Publication-ready quality for an exact target variety/domain without target-specific evaluation.

Lommel, A. R., Uszkoreit, H., & Burchardt, A. (2014). Multidimensional quality metrics (MQM): A framework for declaring and describing translation quality metrics. *Tradumàtica: Tecnologies de la Traducció, 12*, 455–463. https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/tradumatica/tradumatica_a2014n12/tradumatica_a2014n12p455.pdf

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed quality-framework article.
- **Supports:** A structured taxonomy for defining translation error categories and audit profiles.
- **Does not support:** A fixed amount of checking compute, a universal pass threshold, or certification solely from automated scoring.

NLLB Team. (2024). Scaling neural machine translation to 200 languages. *Nature, 630*, 841–846. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07335-x

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical systems paper.
- **Supports:** The reported NLLB-200 evaluation across 200 languages and more than 40,000 directions, including average benchmark improvements under the paper's metrics and datasets.
- **Does not support:** Uniform quality across all directions, domains, scripts, or regional standards; benchmark improvement is not proof of source-faithful publication quality.

Petrov, A., La Malfa, E., Torr, P. H. S., & Bibi, A. (2023). Language model tokenizers introduce unfairness between languages. In *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 36*. https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-1608

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical machine-learning study.
- **Supports:** Substantial cross-language tokenization disparities for evaluated tokenizers, including extreme pair/model cases.
- **Does not support:** Using the maximum observed disparity as the ordinary prior for every corpus or model.

## Partial identification, missing data, and hierarchical estimation

Gelman, A., Carlin, J. B., Stern, H. S., Dunson, D. B., Vehtari, A., & Rubin, D. B. (2013). *Bayesian data analysis* (3rd ed.). CRC Press. https://www.routledge.com/Bayesian-Data-Analysis/Gelman-Carlin-Stern-Dunson-Vehtari-Rubin/p/book/9781439840955

- **Type:** Graduate statistical-methods monograph.
- **Supports:** Joint hierarchical modeling, posterior uncertainty, partial pooling, posterior predictive checking, and sensitivity analysis under explicitly stated probability models.
- **Does not support:** Treating prior-driven estimates as observed facts, using different priors to favor named regions or languages, or concealing model dependence in a published ranking.

Imbens, G. W., & Manski, C. F. (2004). Confidence intervals for partially identified parameters. *Econometrica, 72*(6), 1845–1857. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00555.x

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed econometric methods article.
- **Supports:** Frequentist interval construction when the parameter of interest is only partially identified.
- **Does not support:** A unique point rank when data and assumptions identify only a set, or substitution of a modeled point estimate for a nonparametric identification region.

Little, R. J. A., & Rubin, D. B. (2019). *Statistical analysis with missing data* (3rd ed.). Wiley. https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119482260

- **Type:** Statistical-methods monograph.
- **Supports:** Explicit missing-data mechanisms, likelihood and Bayesian approaches, multiple imputation, diagnostics, and sensitivity to unverifiable assumptions.
- **Does not support:** Coding an unobserved need component as zero, assigning a universal midpoint without a model, or rewarding and penalizing targets according to how much evidence happens to have been collected.

Manski, C. F. (2003). *Partial identification of probability distributions*. Springer. https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/b97478

- **Type:** Econometric methods monograph.
- **Supports:** Reporting identified sets and informative bounds when available evidence does not point-identify a quantity.
- **Does not support:** Fabricating precision to force a complete ordinal ranking or interpreting a wide bound as evidence of low need.

## Mathematics, formal notation, and cross-language comprehension

Cerda, V. R., & Wicha, N. Y. (2024). Arithmetic in the bilingual brain: Language of learning and language experience effects on simple arithmetic in children and adults. *Mind, Brain, and Education, 18*(3), 373–381. https://doi.org/10.1111/mbe.12386

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical study.
- **Supports:** Language-of-learning and language-experience effects in simple arithmetic performance among the studied bilingual children and adults.
- **Does not support:** Technical-text comprehension, interlanguage coverage, or a global effect size for mathematical education.

Daroczy, G., Wolska, M., Meurers, W. D., & Nuerk, H.-C. (2015). Word problems: A review of linguistic and numerical factors contributing to their difficulty. *Frontiers in Psychology, 6*, Article 348. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00348

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed synthesis.
- **Supports:** The interaction of linguistic and numerical factors in mathematical word-problem difficulty.
- **Does not support:** A numerical estimate of the accessibility added by an interlanguage or the claim that mathematical notation neutralizes prose-language differences.

Greisen, M., Georges, C., Hornung, C., Sonnleitner, P., & Schiltz, C. (2021). Learning mathematics with shackles: How lower reading comprehension in the language of mathematics instruction accounts for lower mathematics achievement in speakers of different home languages. *Acta Psychologica, 221*, 103456. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2021.103456

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical study.
- **Supports:** In the studied Luxembourg third-grade cohort, reading comprehension in the language of mathematics instruction statistically mediated home-language group differences in mathematics achievement.
- **Does not support:** A global causal estimate or the proposition that translated text alone removes all mathematical-learning differences.

Österholm, M. (2006). Characterizing reading comprehension of mathematical texts. *Educational Studies in Mathematics, 63*(3), 325–346. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10649-005-9016-y

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical study.
- **Supports:** In a sample of 95 upper-secondary and university students, symbolic mathematical text produced different comprehension behavior and was not simply easier than natural-language-only mathematical text.
- **Does not support:** The blanket claim that formal notation makes unfamiliar-language mathematical prose readily intelligible. It also does not prove the opposite for every expert or genre.

Ott, N., Brünken, R., Vogel, M., & Malone, S. (2018). Multiple symbolic representations: The combination of formula and text supports problem solving in the mathematical field of propositional logic. *Learning and Instruction, 58*, 88–105. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2018.04.010

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical study.
- **Supports:** In the studied unfamiliar-notation tasks, aligned formula and text representations supported propositional-logic problem solving better than a single representation.
- **Does not support:** Formula-only comprehension, cross-language transfer, or a universal benefit from adding prose.

Malone, S., Brünken, R., Vogel, M., & Ott, N. (2020). Homogeneous and heterogeneous multiple representations in equation-solving problems: An eye-tracking study. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 36*, 781–798. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12426

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical study.
- **Supports:** A small performance benefit from multiple familiar equation representations in the studied university sample, with reading time and prior-knowledge differences across representation combinations.
- **Does not support:** A general interlanguage-comprehension multiplier or the proposition that text is always the most efficient companion representation.

Spelke, E. S., & Tsivkin, S. (2001). Language and number: A bilingual training study. *Cognition, 78*(1), 45–88. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(00)00108-6

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed bilingual-training experiment.
- **Supports:** In the studied Russian–English bilingual students, exact numerical information was retrieved more effectively in the training language, while approximate knowledge transferred more readily.
- **Does not support:** A universal bilingual arithmetic effect size, interlanguage comprehension, or language independence of symbol-rich learning.

Hahn, C. G. K., Saalbach, H., & Grabner, R. H. (2019). Language-dependent knowledge acquisition: Investigating bilingual arithmetic learning. *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition, 22*(1), 47–57. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728917000530

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed bilingual arithmetic experiment.
- **Supports:** Language-switching costs after training on multiplication, subtraction, and artificial arithmetic facts in the studied sample.
- **Does not support:** A population-wide cost, a reading-comprehension endpoint, or a claim that all mathematical knowledge remains language-bound.

Hickendorff, M. (2013). The language factor in elementary mathematics assessments: Computational skills and applied problem solving in a multidimensional IRT framework. *Applied Measurement in Education, 26*(4), 253–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2013.824451

- **Type:** Peer-reviewed large-sample assessment analysis.
- **Supports:** Computation and applied problem solving were related but distinct, and language level had a larger association with applied problem solving than with computation in the studied Dutch grades 1–3 sample.
- **Does not support:** A universal stage effect or a direct estimate of translation benefit.

Trakulphadetkrai, N. V., Courtney, L., Clenton, J., Treffers-Daller, J., & Tsakalaki, A. (2020). ## Citation-use rule

No source in this register converts automatically into a rank, population count, compute estimate, or interlanguage coverage multiplier. A publication claim should identify the exact denominator, date, geography, stage, language/variety/script, measurement method, and limitation. Institutional global context and target-specific evidence must remain separate.
